Plainly

What happened with 4.2

4.2 bundled three changes. Two of them were deliberate and defensible on their own. Together, with a nine-year-old defect nobody had reason to look at, they built a one-way door.

Change 1 · deliberate

Routing reweighted toward proximity

Proximity 0.45 → 0.60. Recent-acceptance 0.40 → 0.25. A long-standing, deliberate ask. Not a bug.

Change 2 · deliberate

Callout timeout cut 90s → 60s

Thirty fewer seconds to reach the phone. Committed in the Q3 roadmap as "ping timeout tuning."

The part nobody shipped

A missed phone scores as a refusal

And the score never recovers on its own. An open TODO from 2019 that 4.2 turned from harmless into load-bearing.

In one sentence: 4.2 didn't break routing — it narrowed the escape route from a trap that was already in the code. Fewer offers, less time to answer each one, and a score that only goes back up by answering one.

The inherited read

"It's mostly seasonal. August is always soft."

Total weekly volume was flat-to-peak right through release week and only dropped the week after. And a seasonal dip doesn't create winners — this was redistribution, not softening.

The file covers one season, so it can't confirm or refute "August is always soft" as a general claim either. It can rule it out as this driver.

What was actually true

Two different complaints, one story

"Offer gone before I could answer" was expected and explained — the timeout cut. "Phone never rings" was the unexplained two-thirds. They are the same mechanism, one step apart: miss the 60-second window, lose score, stop getting offers.

Evidence · 00-rook/data/callout-history.csv

The headline metric recovered on its own

Acceptance rate is Dispatch's headline number, reported weekly in aggregate. It crashed the week 4.2 shipped and climbed back for three straight weeks — with no code shipped after 4.2. I checked the changelog.

Weekly aggregate acceptance rate (offers taken ÷ offers sent, all 16 responders) 4.2 ships, 12 Aug

Why it recovered

Self-sorting. The reweight pulls fast, reliable responders to the top, so fewer offers get wasted on cascade hops. The average improves because the same few people take everything.

And behaviour adapted

Aunt Dot now tells Vesper's partner to keep the phone in his pocket rather than upstairs, to beat the 60-second window. People fixed it themselves, uncredited.

The trap in the number

Recovery here is evidence of the problem, not evidence against it. A metric that improves when concentration gets worse will never show you concentration.

Evidence · the same CSV, split by person

Underneath it, the work moved

Same ten weeks. Same file. Offers sent per responder per week. Five responders rise every single week after 4.2. Four collapse toward zero and keep going. Nothing in the aggregate shows this.

Rising: The Gale, Nightwell, Stormwrack, Captain Vantage, Sgt. Falkirk Collapsing: Farlight, Meteor Mite, The Undertow, Vesper Everyone else

Still getting worse, week over week

This is the part that didn't recover. Farlight went 12 → 10 → 3 → 1 → 0. The Gale went 13 → 15 → 17 → 20 → 21. Both of these are the same release working as designed.

It isn't geography

The four moved in a synchronised, monotonic pattern — not the noisy up-and-down you'd get from geographic luck. And Meteor Mite and The Gale have the same handler, same city, same weeks, moving in opposite directions.

Root cause · confirmed in 00-rook/code/dispatch-routing/

The one-way door, in nine lines of code

config.py
# How long a callout offer stays on a responder's
# phone before we give up and move to the next person.
OFFER_TIMEOUT_SECONDS = 60      # was 90 until 4.2

WEIGHT_PROXIMITY         = 0.60  # was 0.45 until 4.2
WEIGHT_RECENT_ACCEPTANCE = 0.25  # was 0.40 until 4.2
WEIGHT_CAPABILITY_MATCH  = 0.15  # unchanged since 4.0

ACCEPTANCE_CREDIT = 0.08
DECLINE_PENALTY   = 0.12

The penalty is 1.5× the credit. You need three accepted callouts to undo two missed ones — and there is no reset or migration logic anywhere. Everyone's existing score carried straight across the release boundary.

history.py
# TODO(wen, 2019): should this ease back toward
# NEUTRAL_SCORE on its own after a while? For: somebody
# who had a bad month shouldn't still be carrying it in
# the spring. Against: if somebody has stopped taking
# work, we probably want that to stick until they take
# work again. Leaving it as-is for now.
def record_declined(responder):
    """They turned it down, or we ran out of time
    waiting. Same either way — we asked and we
    didn't get a yes."""
    _set(responder, recent_acceptance(responder) - DECLINE_PENALTY)

A phone in another room is recorded identically to a refusal. And the only function that puts points back on is record_accepted() — which requires landing an offer inside a 60-second window you're no longer being sent.

The loop: miss an offer → score drops → rank drops → fewer offers → fewer chances to earn it back → the rare offer that does arrive is more likely to time out → score drops further. Nothing in the system reverses this. The only exits are a very close incident, every higher-ranked responder declining first, a handler's manual override, or an engineering change.

The fix · 05-super-speed/

Standing — what we'd build instead of changing a constant

Ship today · decoupled

Stop scoring a missed phone as a refusal

It's a real defect, it's correct on its own merits, and it depends on nothing below. My first draft held it hostage to the bigger proposal — that was me protecting the proposal over the product. Let it go this afternoon.

Piece 1 · ships first, carries most of the value

Standing, in plain words, with the reason

The coverage card stops being a ping count and becomes an explanation: "Meteor Mite is being ranked below most nearby responders. Six missed offers since 10 Aug. Standing is low and isn't recovering on its own."

Piece 2

A guaranteed look

Two weeks under threshold and Dispatch sends the next suitable callout regardless of rank — at 90 seconds, not 60, because they're cold. Not automatic decay, not permanence. You still earn it back; we make sure you get the chance to.

Piece 3

Tell the responder on their phone

A quiet week you understand is a completely different experience from a quiet week you don't.

Why this and not a decay constant: a constant fixes Mite's number and teaches us nothing. The next time routing re-ranks someone into silence — mutual aid in Q4 will do exactly this — we'd be back here with no visibility and no recovery path. Standing survives the next reweight.

The honest list

What building it surfaced that writing it never would

These are not caveats. Three of them are reasons the proposal isn't ready, and one of them is a prerequisite that outranks the whole thing.

Blocker · Marcus, before anything

We can't prove an offer was ever delivered

push_to_device() returns nothing. Dispatch knows only that it sent. Every "N offers reached you" in my prototype is a claim the system cannot make — and if Nightwell and Ironvale are delivery faults, standing shows them a well-designed lie.

Counter-intuitive · have this ready

Capping the busiest responder helps the quietest by zero

Throttle The Gale and the overflow cascades to the next-ranked high-standing responders — Falkirk, Stormwrack, Vantage, Nightwell. Meteor Mite, 14th of 16, gets +0. It's the first fix anyone proposes and it silently fails.

Unpriced

A guaranteed look is taken from the incident

Mite at 34 minutes instead of someone at 6, plus 90 seconds before the cascade. That's a safety trade and I costed it at zero. It also lowers acceptance rate on purpose — Ravi's headline weekly number. Needs a segmented metric or an explicit agreement.

Scale & scope

My own threshold flags 4 of 16 at once

That's a second routing policy, not a safety valve, and contention between competing guaranteed looks is undefined. The 90s also partially reverses a committed 4.2 decision — Helen's call, not an implementation detail.

Undefined

"The next callout Mite can do" isn't expressible

capability_score is partial credit, rank_for_callout never filters ("Nobody is removed here"), and callouts carry no severity field. This is a data-model change, not a config change.

Open questions · still unanswered

Two data-integrity anomalies, and Wen Li

Nightwell and Ironvale both filed "no callouts" tickets during the exact weeks the backend shows them at record volume — likely notification delivery, not routing. And Marcus's 14 Aug question to Wen Li has never been answered. Messages drafted, not yet sent.

Meteor Mite has no pronoun in any source. Kip says "Mite thinks Mite's been forgotten" — the name twice, never "he" or "she." Cover identity is never stored or derivable. Earlier drafts of mine invented one; corrected in both deliverables.

So it doesn't happen again

The habit that catches the next one

4.2 wasn't a bad decision. It was three reasonable decisions shipped together, with nobody holding the question "who does this make worse?" The fix for that isn't vigilance. It's a check that runs whether or not I remember to run it.

06-sidekicks/review-checklist.md · runs every Monday morning
  • Owner named — not "the team," a person.
  • Success defined — a metric, a signal, a threshold. Not a description of the fix.
  • Scope holds — the scope at the end matches the scope at the start.
  • Problem before fix — flag any brief that opens with the solution and backfills the problem.

Point it at any brief and get the same four questions, every time, without re-explaining them. Scheduled review-checklist-weekly, Monday mornings, against whatever briefs are in the directory. Nothing has to be ready for it to fire — the point is the habit, not the result.

First run · five briefs · what it found
3 of 5 failed the same check

No real success measure stated up front

Bulk Callout and Handler Phone App had something directional but no baseline. Routing Override Audit Log had none at all. And my own Standing brief buries the metric question inside "Open questions" rather than stating it.

  • Handler Phone App states the fix before the problem — Proposal section sits above "What this solves."
  • Requisition Approval Chains scope-creeps inside its own proposal: "that's the whole ask," immediately followed by four more asks.
  • The useful part: this is a pattern across the team's briefs, not a one-off. A one-off you fix. A pattern you build a check for.

The check caught its author. That's the only real evidence it works — I'm rereading the Standing brief with this lens before it goes to Helen.
