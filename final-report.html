01 The recommendation

Make standing visible.
Guarantee a way back.

Engineering can fix the number this afternoon. That's worth doing and it isn't the answer — it leaves the next reweight to find the same cliff with the same blindfold on.

  1. ship today

    Stop scoring a missed phone as a refusal

    A timeout and a decline take the same points off. That's a defect, it's correct on its own merits, and it depends on nothing below. Decoupled — it goes this afternoon, not bundled into the proposal.

  2. most of the value

    Standing, in plain words, on the handler's card

    Not a ping count. "Meteor Mite is ranked below most nearby responders. Six missed offers since 10 Aug. Standing is low and isn't recovering on its own." Kip asked for it to "look less like a coincidence at midnight." This is that.

  3. A guaranteed look

    Two weeks below standing with almost no offers, and Dispatch sends the next suitable callout regardless of rank — at 90 seconds, not 60, because they're cold. Take it, normal credit applies, you climb out. Not automatic decay: you still earn it back, but we make sure you get the chance to.

  4. Tell the responder

    Today Mite's phone says nothing, so Mite texts the handler to ask if the product is broken. A quiet week you understand is a different experience from a quiet week you don't.

prerequisite

Before any of it: delivery confirmation

push_to_device() returns nothing. Dispatch knows it sent, never that it arrived. Every "11 offers reached you" is a claim the system can't make — and if the two anomalies below are delivery faults, standing would show them a beautifully designed lie. Marcus, first.

03 What happened

Three changes shipped together on 12 August.

Each was defensible. Nobody modelled them as a system.

change one

Rank by distance

proximity 0.45 → 0.60
acceptance 0.40 → 0.25

A long-standing, deliberate ask. Not a bug.

change two

Less time to answer

timeout 90s → 60s

Cut cascade latency. Also cut the window for anyone whose phone isn't in their hand.

change three

Filter persistence

Cosmetic. Generated noise tickets that buried the signal in the support queue for two weeks.

Within a fortnight, tickets tripled. Two thirds of them said "my phone never rings" — which nothing in the release notes predicted or explained.

04 Finding one

The average lied by telling the truth.

Acceptance rate cratered on release week, then climbed back three weeks running — with zero code changes shipped. Read on its own, it says "shock, recovering, stand down."

~77%six weeks before
54%release week
73%three weeks later

The recovery is real, and it's caused by the problem. Routing self-sorts: reliable responders rise, wasted cascade hops fall, the ratio improves. The metric gets healthier precisely because the work is concentrating.

05 Finding two

Underneath it, the work moved.

Total volume barely dipped. It just stopped going to the same people. Five responders climb every week. Four fall off a cliff.

This is not seasonal softening. Seasonal softening doesn't create winners. And it isn't geography: Meteor Mite and The Gale share a handler, a city and a calendar, and they moved in opposite directions.

def record_declined(responder):
    """They turned it down, or we ran out of
    time waiting. Same either way."""
    _set(responder,
         recent_acceptance(responder) - DECLINE_PENALTY)

One way down, one way up

Points come off for a decline and for a phone you didn't reach in 60 seconds. Points only go on by accepting an offer — and the trap is that fewer offers arrive.

The score never decays back to neutral. Wen flagged it in 2019 and left it, for a good reason: someone who really stopped working shouldn't quietly re-rank. 4.2 turned that reasonable choice into a one-way door.

Keep clicking "Miss one". The button that fixes it disables itself.

08 How I found it

Six questions, in order.

Verbatim. The useful ones were short, specific, and willing to be wrong.

01 · don't accept the framing
is fixing the issues from the 4.2 release the most prominent thing I should focus on as a PM?
Answer: no. It reframed the first month as four parallel tracks — a bounded 4.2 investigation, relationships, the missing routing spec, and Q4. Everything downstream stayed scoped because of this.
02 · look for the disagreement
You've now read both folders. Where do they disagree? What's loud in the interviews but rare in the tickets, and what's all over the tickets that nobody brought up in the interviews?
Answer: they're disjoint populations. One name overlaps across 25 tickets and 4 interviews. Tickets skew angry; the interviews were UX research and undersold the severity for the worst-affected. Neither source could have found this alone.
03 · state the hypothesis, invite the correction
what causes this redistribution? is there a respondent score that causes loser cluster is not getting pings again because their scores have dropped and they not getting pings causes the score don't raise again and making this losing effect persistent?
Answer: yes — and it was confirmable in code, not inferred. Naming the suspected loop made it checkable. The same pass also killed the "mostly seasonal" read: volume peaked during release week.

09 How I found it — continued

Then: prove it, build it, keep it from recurring.

04 · ask for the asymmetry, not the explanation
Find me the part of this code that takes points off somebody when they miss a ping or turn one down. Show it to me and explain it in plain English. Then find me every single thing in this code that puts points back on.
Answer: two ways down, one way up, no decay. Asking for the complete list of the opposite force is what exposed the one-way door. "Explain the scoring" would have produced a summary and nothing actionable.
05 · refuse the description
Take the brief you just wrote and build me a working prototype, an actual screen I can click through, not a description of one. Save it as 05-super-speed/prototype.html, a single file I can just open in my browser. Show me where this would actually happen, and make at least one thing on it respond when I click it.
Answer: the screen two slides up. Building it surfaced three things the brief alone never would have — the delivery-confirmation blocker, that capping the busiest responder reaches the quietest one by exactly zero extra offers, and that "the next callout Mite can do" isn't a concept routing has.
06 · turn the habit into a thing that runs
Turn my review habit into a reusable skill: owner named, success measure stated, scope at the end matches scope at the start, problem explained before the fix. Save it in this directory and schedule it weekly.
Answer: it failed my own brief first. See the next slide.

10 So it doesn't happen again

Four questions, every brief, every Monday.

4.2 wasn't a coding failure. Three reasonable changes shipped with no stated success measure that would have caught the fourth thing they did together. So I made the check automatic.

the checklist
  • Owner named. Not "the team." A person.
  • Success defined. A metric, a signal, a threshold — not a description of the fix.
  • Scope holds. Does the scope at the end match the scope at the start?
  • Problem before fix. Flag any brief that opens with the solution and backfills.

Runs weekly against every brief in the directory. Returns pass/fail with a one-line reason — not a rewrite.

first run · five briefs

It found a pattern, not a typo

3 of 5 had no real success measure. Two were directional with no baseline. One had none at all.

One stated the fix before the problem. One scope-crept inside its own proposal — "that's the whole ask," immediately followed by four more asks.

Including mine

My Standing brief buries its success measure inside "Open questions" instead of stating it up front. The check caught the author. That's the point — a habit you have to remember isn't a habit.

11 The honest list

What I don't know.

A proposal with no open questions is a proposal nobody stress-tested.

  • Blocker. No delivery confirmation. Two responders — Nightwell and Ironvale — filed "no callouts" tickets during the exact weeks the backend shows them at peak volume. If that's a delivery fault, standing would show them a well-designed lie. Marcus. Prerequisite.
  • Unpriced. A guaranteed look is taken from the incident — Mite at 34 minutes instead of someone at 6, plus 90 seconds before cascade. That's a safety trade and I've costed it at zero.
  • Scale. My own threshold flags 4 of 16 at once. That's a second routing policy, not a safety valve. Contention between competing guaranteed looks is undefined.
  • Undefined. "The next callout Mite can do" isn't expressible: capability is partial credit, ranking never filters, callouts carry no severity. Data-model change, not a config change.
  • Cost. This lowers acceptance rate on purpose — the headline weekly number. Needs a segmented metric or an explicit agreement.
  • Scope. The 90 seconds partially reverses a committed 4.2 decision. Helen's call, not an implementation detail.
  • Untested. Neither Kip nor any gone-quiet responder has seen this. Mite's phone screen is my invention of how that week feels.
  • Not asked. Wen hasn't seen it, and it answers her 2019 note by name.

And one thing I got wrong

My first draft held the arithmetic fix back so it could ship alongside the proposal. That was me protecting the proposal over the product. It goes on its own, today.
